I wrote the clustering notes while I was employed as an actuarial analyst, in a role that used these algorithmic measures. The same family of methods that splits a cloud of points is used to split people. Credit, insurance, hiring, and housing group observations by distance, by a chosen number of centers, and by whatever columns are on the file.

I stand for human rights. I stand for the rights of U.S. citizens against algorithmic bias and discrimination. Publishing the mechanics is part of that work. A reader should be able to see when a model follows a stand-in for a protected trait, when scaling moves a person into another group, when a mean is pulled by an extreme point, and when a local minimum freezes a bad partition.

This is support for those rights. I am not a protester against politics.

The issues, as I keep them next to the formulas:

- Equal treatment when an algorithm sorts people in credit, insurance, hiring, housing, education, healthcare, or the justice system.
- The rights of U.S. citizens, and human rights more broadly, including civil-rights protections already named in U.S. law.
- A clear view of proxy discrimination, sampling bias, and disparate impact, including when a protected characteristic never appears as a column.
- Remedies that can be used in a real case: a less discriminatory alternative, a way to measure the harm, and a path through missing data and black-box scores.
- The people who bear the harm have to be the starting point, not an afterthought on a fairness metric.

The clustering page holds the mathematics and short summaries of the research I point to. I did not write those papers.
